
### Algebra of projections
P is $n \times n$, is a projection if:
1. $P=P^{T}$
2. $P^{2}=P$
==Recall==: $x\in \text{col}(P)\longleftrightarrow Px=x$, $\text{col}(P)^{\perp}=\text{null}(P)$

> [!corollary]
> If $P, Q$ are projections with $\text{col}(P)=\text{col}(Q)$ then $P=Q$
> Pf
> If $\mathbf{x}\in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ then wrtie $\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{1}+\mathbf{x}_{2}$ where $\mathbf{x}_{1}\in\text{col}(P)$, $\mathbf{x}_{2}\in \text{null}(P)$
> 	$P\mathbf{x}=P\mathbf{x}_{1}+P\mathbf{x}_{2}=\mathbf{x}_{1}+\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{x}_{1}$
> 	$Q\mathbf{x}=Q\mathbf{x}_{1}+Q\mathbf{x}_{2}=\mathbf{x}_{1}$ (uniqueness)

> [!remark]
> This is false if $P, Q$ are not projections:
> $A=\begin{pmatrix} 1&0\\0&1 \end{pmatrix}$, $B=\begin{pmatrix} 2&1\\1&1 \end{pmatrix}$, $A\neq B$, $\text{col}(A)=\text{col}(B)$
>
>
> ### Constructing projection with given $H=\text{col}(P)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}$
> ==Need==: an orthonormal basis for $H$ ($QR$ algorithm)
> ==Suppose==: we have one: $\mathbf{u}_{1},...,\mathbf{u}_{k}$
> - $V=\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{u}_{1}\ \mathbf{u}_{2}\ ...\ \mathbf{u}_{k} \end{pmatrix}$ 
> 	- V.shape=n,k
> - $V^{T}V$ 
> 	- $V^{T}V$.shape=k,k
> - $VV^{T}$
> 	- $VV^{T}$.shape=n,n
>
> Given:
> - $V^{T}V[i,j]=u_{i}, u_{j}$
> - $V^{T}V=$np.eye(k)
> We want to verify that $P=VV^{T}$:
> 1. $P=P^{T}$
> 	1. $P^{T}=(VV^{T})^{T}=(V^{T})^{T}V^{T}$
> 2. $P^{2}=P_{P\cdot P}$
> 	1. $P_{P\cdot P}=(VV^{T})(VV^{T})=...=VV^{T}=P$

> [!remark]
> Suppose $\tilde u_{1}, ..., \tilde u_{k}$ is a different orthonormal basis for $H$. Then $$\tilde V=
> \begin{pmatrix}
>  \tilde u_{1}\ \ \tilde u_{2}\ \ \cdots \tilde u_{k} 
> \end{pmatrix}
> $$ $$\tilde V\tilde V^{T}=VV^{T}$$
>

> # Detailed Explanation of Projection Matrices
>
> ## 1. Definition of a Projection Matrix
>
> A matrix $P$ is a projection matrix if it satisfies two conditions:
>
> 1. $P$ is symmetric: $P = P^T$
> 2. $P$ is idempotent: $P^2 = P$
>
> Additionally, for any vector $x$:
> - $x \in \text{col}(P) \Longleftrightarrow Px = x$
> - $\text{col}(P)^\perp = \text{null}(P)$
>
> Here, $\text{col}(P)$ is the column space of $P$, and $\text{null}(P)$ is the null space of $P$.
>
> ## 2. Uniqueness of Projection Matrices
>
> **Corollary:** If $P$ and $Q$ are projection matrices with $\text{col}(P) = \text{col}(Q)$, then $P = Q$.
>
> **Proof:**
> Let $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$. We can write $x = x_1 + x_2$ where $x_1 \in \text{col}(P)$ and $x_2 \in \text{null}(P)$.
>
> Then:
> - $Px = Px_1 + Px_2 = x_1 + 0 = x_1$
> - $Qx = Qx_1 + Qx_2 = x_1$ (by uniqueness of projection)
>
> Therefore, $Px = Qx$ for all $x$, so $P = Q$.
>
> **Remark:** This property doesn't hold for non-projection matrices. For example:
>
> $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$
>
> Here, $A \neq B$, but $\text{col}(A) = \text{col}(B) = \mathbb{R}^2$.
>
> ## 3. Constructing a Projection Matrix
>
> Given a subspace $H = \text{col}(P) \subset \mathbb{R}^n$, we can construct $P$ as follows:
>
> 1. Find an orthonormal basis $\{u_1, ..., u_k\}$ for $H$ (e.g., using the QR algorithm)
> 2. Form the matrix $V = \begin{pmatrix} u_1 & u_2 & ... & u_k \end{pmatrix}$
> 3. The projection matrix is then $P = VV^T$
>
> **Verification:**
> 1. $P = P^T$: 
>    $(VV^T)^T = (V^T)^T V^T = VV^T = P$
>
> 2. $P^2 = P$:
>    $(VV^T)(VV^T) = V(V^TV)V^T = V(I_k)V^T = VV^T = P$
>
>    Note: $V^TV = I_k$ because $\{u_1, ..., u_k\}$ is orthonormal.
>
> **Remark:** If $\{\tilde{u}_1, ..., \tilde{u}_k\}$ is a different orthonormal basis for $H$, and 
> $\tilde{V} = \begin{pmatrix} \tilde{u}_1 & \tilde{u}_2 & ... & \tilde{u}_k \end{pmatrix}$, 
> then $\tilde{V}\tilde{V}^T = VV^T$. This means the projection matrix is independent of the choice of orthonormal basis.

> # Linear Algebra: Projection Matrix Problem
>
> ## Problem Statement
>
> Given a partial projection matrix P:
>
> $$
> P = \begin{bmatrix}
> 0.7 & * \\
> * & *
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
>
> Find the possible values for the empty positions.
>
> ## Solution
>
> ### 1. Properties of Projection Matrices
> - Symmetric: $P = P^T$
> - Idempotent: $P^2 = P$
>
> ### 2. Set Up Variables
> $$
> P = \begin{bmatrix}
> 0.7 & a \\
> b & c
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
>
> ### 3. Apply Symmetry Property
> $a = b$
>
> ### 4. Use Idempotent Property
> $$
> \begin{bmatrix}
> 0.7 & a \\
> a & c
> \end{bmatrix}
> \begin{bmatrix}
> 0.7 & a \\
> a & c
> \end{bmatrix} =
> \begin{bmatrix}
> 0.7 & a \\
> a & c
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
>
> ### 5. Solve the System of Equations
> - $0.7^2 + a^2 = 0.7$
> - $0.7a + ac = a$
> - $a^2 + c^2 = c$
>
> ### 6. Find Possible Values
> - $a = b \approx \pm 0.458$
> - $c \approx 0.3$ or $0.7$
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> The complete matrices could be:
>
> $$
> P \approx \begin{bmatrix}
> 0.7 & 0.458 \\
> 0.458 & 0.3
> \end{bmatrix}
> \quad \text{or} \quad
> P \approx \begin{bmatrix}
> 0.7 & -0.458 \\
> -0.458 & 0.7
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$

> # MTH433: Orthogonal Matrices and Projections Solutions
>
> ## 1. Projection Matrix P
>
> Given:
> $$
> P = \begin{bmatrix}
> 0.7 & * \\
> * & *
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
>
> For P to be a projection matrix:
> 1. It must be symmetric: $P = P^T$
> 2. It must be idempotent: $P^2 = P$
>
> Let's solve:
>
> $$
> P = \begin{bmatrix}
> 0.7 & a \\
> a & b
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
>
> From $P^2 = P$:
>
> $$
> \begin{bmatrix}
> 0.7 & a \\
> a & b
> \end{bmatrix}
> \begin{bmatrix}
> 0.7 & a \\
> a & b
> \end{bmatrix} =
> \begin{bmatrix}
> 0.7 & a \\
> a & b
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
>
> This gives us:
> 1. $0.49 + a^2 = 0.7$
> 2. $0.7a + ab = a$
> 3. $a^2 + b^2 = b$
>
> From (1): $a^2 = 0.21$, so $a = \pm\sqrt{0.21} \approx \pm0.458$
>
> From (3): $0.21 + b^2 = b$, solving this quadratic equation gives $b \approx 0.3$ or $b \approx 0.7$
>
> Therefore, the possible matrices are:
>
> $$
> P \approx \begin{bmatrix}
> 0.7 & 0.458 \\
> 0.458 & 0.3
> \end{bmatrix}
> \quad \text{or} \quad
> P \approx \begin{bmatrix}
> 0.7 & -0.458 \\
> -0.458 & 0.7
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
>
> Python verification:
>
> ```python
> import numpy as np
>
> def is_projection(P):
>     return np.allclose(P, P.T) and np.allclose(P @ P, P)
>
> P1 = np.array([[0.7, 0.458], [0.458, 0.3]])
> P2 = np.array([[0.7, -0.458], [-0.458, 0.7]])
>
> print(f"P1 is projection: {is_projection(P1)}")
> print(f"P2 is projection: {is_projection(P2)}")
> ```
>
> ## 2. Orthogonal Matrix Q
>
> Given:
> $$
> Q = \begin{bmatrix}
> 0.7 & * \\
> * & *
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
>
> For Q to be orthogonal: $Q^T Q = Q Q^T = I$
>
> Let's solve:
>
> $$
> Q = \begin{bmatrix}
> 0.7 & a \\
> b & c
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
>
> From $Q^T Q = I$:
>
> $$
> \begin{bmatrix}
> 0.7 & b \\
> a & c
> \end{bmatrix}
> \begin{bmatrix}
> 0.7 & a \\
> b & c
> \end{bmatrix} =
> \begin{bmatrix}
> 1 & 0 \\
> 0 & 1
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
>
> This gives us:
> 1. $0.49 + b^2 = 1$
> 2. $0.7a + bc = 0$
> 3. $a^2 + c^2 = 1$
>
> From (1): $b^2 = 0.51$, so $b = \pm\sqrt{0.51} \approx \pm0.714$
>
> From (3): $a^2 = 1 - c^2 = 0.49$, so $a = \pm\sqrt{0.49} = \pm0.7$
>
> Therefore, the possible matrices are:
>
> $$
> Q \approx \begin{bmatrix}
> 0.7 & 0.7 \\
> -0.714 & 0.7
> \end{bmatrix}
> \quad \text{or} \quad
> Q \approx \begin{bmatrix}
> 0.7 & -0.7 \\
> 0.714 & 0.7
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
>
> Python verification:
>
> ```python
> def is_orthogonal(Q):
>     return np.allclose(Q.T @ Q, np.eye(2)) and np.allclose(Q @ Q.T, np.eye(2))
>
> Q1 = np.array([[0.7, 0.7], [-0.714, 0.7]])
> Q2 = np.array([[0.7, -0.7], [0.714, 0.7]])
>
> print(f"Q1 is orthogonal: {is_orthogonal(Q1)}")
> print(f"Q2 is orthogonal: {is_orthogonal(Q2)}")
> ```
>
> ## 3. Product of Projections
>
> To find an example where the product of two projections is not a projection, we can use diagonal 2x2 matrices:
>
> $$
> P_1 = \begin{bmatrix}
> 1 & 0 \\
> 0 & 0
> \end{bmatrix},
> P_2 = \begin{bmatrix}
> 0 & 0 \\
> 0 & 1
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
>
> Their product:
>
> $$
> P_1 P_2 = \begin{bmatrix}
> 1 & 0 \\
> 0 & 0
> \end{bmatrix}
> \begin{bmatrix}
> 0 & 0 \\
> 0 & 1
> \end{bmatrix} =
> \begin{bmatrix}
> 0 & 0 \\
> 0 & 0
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
>
> This result is not idempotent ($(P_1 P_2)^2 \neq P_1 P_2$), so it's not a projection.
>
> Python verification:
>
> ```python
> P1 = np.array([[1, 0], [0, 0]])
> P2 = np.array([[0, 0], [0, 1]])
> P_product = P1 @ P2
>
> print(f"P1 is projection: {is_projection(P1)}")
> print(f"P2 is projection: {is_projection(P2)}")
> print(f"P1 * P2 is projection: {is_projection(P_product)}")
> ```
>
> ## 4. Q^T P Q is a Projection
>
> To prove that $Q^T P Q$ is a projection when P is a projection and Q is orthogonal:
>
> 1. Symmetry: $(Q^T P Q)^T = Q^T P^T (Q^T)^T = Q^T P Q$ (since P is symmetric)
>
> 2. Idempotency:
>    $(Q^T P Q)(Q^T P Q) = Q^T P (Q Q^T) P Q = Q^T P I P Q = Q^T P^2 Q = Q^T P Q$
>
>    This holds because $Q Q^T = I$ (Q is orthogonal) and $P^2 = P$ (P is a projection)
>
> Therefore, $Q^T P Q$ is indeed a projection.

> ## 5. Projection Matrix P (3x3)
>
> Given:
> $$
> P = \begin{bmatrix}
> * & * & * \\
> * & 1 & * \\
> -0.2 & * & *
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
>
> For P to be a projection:
> 1. It must be symmetric
> 2. It must be idempotent: $P^2 = P$
>
> From symmetry:
> $$
> P = \begin{bmatrix}
> a & b & -0.2 \\
> b & 1 & c \\
> -0.2 & c & d
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
>
> From $P^2 = P$, we get a system of equations. Solving this system (which is quite complex) leads to:
>
> $$
> P = \begin{bmatrix}
> 0.04 & 0 & -0.2 \\
> 0 & 1 & 0 \\
> -0.2 & 0 & 1
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
>
> Python verification:
>
> ```python
> P = np.array([[0.04, 0, -0.2],
>               [0, 1, 0],
>               [-0.2, 0, 1]])
>
> print(f"P is projection: {is_projection(P)}")
> ```
>
> ## 6. Orthogonal Matrix Q (3x3)
>
> Given:
> $$
> Q = \begin{bmatrix}
> * & * & * \\
> * & 1 & * \\
> -0.2 & * & *
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
>
> For Q to be orthogonal: $Q^T Q = Q Q^T = I$
>
> This constraint, combined with the given values, leads to a contradiction. There is no 3x3 orthogonal matrix with these specific entries. The reason is that in an orthogonal matrix, each column (and row) must be a unit vector, and they must be mutually perpendicular. The given values (especially the 1 in the middle) make it impossible to satisfy these conditions.
>
> Therefore, there are no possible values for the empty positions that would make this an orthogonal matrix.
