### Every symmetric matrix has a real eigenvalue
*ex.* $A=\begin{pmatrix} 2&0\\0&-3 \end{pmatrix}$
- $\text{det}(A-\lambda)=(\lambda-2)(\lambda+3)$
- $\lambda=2,-3$ are both eigenvalues
*ex.* $A=\begin{pmatrix} a&b\\b&d \end{pmatrix}$
- $(\lambda-a)(\lambda-d)-b^{2}$
	- $=\lambda^{2}-(a+d)\lambda+ad-b^{2}=0$
- $\lambda=\frac{...\pm\sqrt{...}}{...}$ (quadratic)
- $A$ has a real eigenvalue if and only if:
	- $(a+d)^{2}-4(ad-b^{2})\ge 0$
	- $= a^{2}+2ad+d^{2}-4ad+4b^{2}$
	- $=(a-d)^{2}+4b^{2}\ge 0$
	- ... this method doesn't generalize to higher dimensions
If we did a little geometry, we'd see that a $2 \times 2$ symmetric matrix [[Drawing 2024-10-30 11.50.30.excalidraw|does generate.]] 
==Recall==. Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality: $$\exists x,y\in  \mathbb{R}^{n}\ \ \ x\cdot y\leq \lvert\lvert x \rvert\rvert \ \lvert\lvert y \rvert\rvert $$... when $x\cdot y=\lvert\lvert x \rvert\rvert\ \lvert\lvert y \rvert\rvert$, we have: $$x=\left( \frac{x\cdot y}{\lvert\lvert y \rvert\rvert ^{2}} \right)y$$ ... where $x=\left( x\cdot y \right)y$ if $\lvert\lvert y \rvert\rvert=1$.
#### Why?
*Why does every symmetric matrix have a real eigenvalue?*
- Get $\mathbf{x}^{*}$ with $\lvert\lvert x^{*} \rvert\rvert=1$, so :$$M=\lvert\lvert A\mathbf{x}^{*} \rvert\rvert =\text{max}\left\{ \lvert\lvert A\mathbf{y} \rvert\rvert :\mathbf{y}\in  \mathbb{R}^{n}, \lvert\lvert y \rvert\rvert =1 \right\}$$
- *Claim.* $M$ or $-M$ is an eigenvalue
	1. $y=\frac{A\mathbf{x}^{*}}{\lvert\lvert A\mathbf{x}^{*} \rvert\rvert}$ ... $\leftrightarrow A\mathbf{x}^{*}=M\mathbf{y}$
	2. $M=A\mathbf{x}^{*}\cdot \mathbf{y}\le \lvert\lvert \mathbf{x}^{*} \rvert\rvert\ \lvert\lvert A\mathbf{y} \rvert\rvert\leq 1\cdot M$
		1. only place we use $A=A^{T}$
	3. $A\mathbf{y}=M\mathbf{x}^{*}$
		1. **Case**: $\mathbf{x}^{*}=y$
			1. Then $A\mathbf{x}^{*}=M\mathbf{x}^{*}$
		2. **Case**: $\mathbf{x}^{*}\longrightarrow \mathbf{x}^{*}-\mathbf{y}\neq \mathbf{0}$
			1. $A(\mathbf{x}^{*}-y)=A\mathbf{x}^{*}-A\mathbf{y}=M\mathbf{y}-M\mathbf{x}^{*}$
			2. $= -M(\mathbf{x}^{*}-y)$

*ex.* $A=\begin{pmatrix} -3&2\\2&-3 \end{pmatrix}$
1. Calculate $\mathbf{x}^{*}$
2. Let $y=\begin{pmatrix} y_{1}\\y_{2} \end{pmatrix}$ have magnitude $1$ ($y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}=1$)
3. Maximize $\lvert\lvert A\mathbf{y} \rvert\rvert=\lvert \lvert \begin{pmatrix} -3&2\\2&-3 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} y_{1}\\y_{2} \end{pmatrix} \rvert \rvert=\lvert\lvert \begin{pmatrix} -3y_{1}+2y_{2}\\2y_{1}-3y_{2} \end{pmatrix} \rvert\rvert$
	1. $=\sqrt{9y_{1}^{2}-12y_{1}y_{2}+4y_{2}^{2}+4y_{1}^{2}-12y_{1}y_{2}+9y_{2}^{2}}$
	2. $=\sqrt{13-24y_{1}y_{2}}$
4. Find $\begin{pmatrix} y_{1}\\y_{2} \end{pmatrix}$ such that $-y_{1}, y_{2}$ is maximized.
	1. Guess $\mathbf{x}^{*}=\begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\\-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \end{pmatrix}$
	2. Then $M=\lvert\lvert A\mathbf{x}^{*} \rvert\rvert=\sqrt{13-24\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \right)\left( -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \right)}=\sqrt{13+12}$
5. Check of $\mathbf{x}^{*}$ is an eigenvector ... evaluate $\pm 5$.
	1. $A\mathbf{x}^{*}=\begin{pmatrix} -3&2\\2&-3 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\\-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix} -\frac{5}{\sqrt{2}} \\ \frac{5}{\sqrt{2}}\end{pmatrix}=-5\begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\\-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \end{pmatrix}$
#### Key Fact
Suppose $A$ is $n \times n$ symmetric:
- If $\lambda\neq M$ are both eigenvalues for $A$ and $A\mathbf{x}=\lambda x_{1}, A\mathbf{y}=M\mathbf{y}$, then $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ are orthogonal.
*Why?*
- WLOG, $\lambda\ne 0$
- $\mathbf{x}\cdot \mathbf{y}=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda}\mathbf{x};\cdot \mathbf{y}$
	- $=\frac{1}{\lambda}(\lambda \mathbf{x}\cdot \mathbf{y})$
	- $=\frac{1}{\lambda}(A\mathbf{x}\cdot \mathbf{y})$
	- $= \frac{1}{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}\cdot A\mathbf{y})$
- $\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}=\frac{\lambda}{M}\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}$
- ... so $\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}=0$ or $\frac{\lambda}{M}=1$
	- latter case impossible
	- thus $\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}=0$

*ex.* $A=\begin{pmatrix} 1&2\\0&-1 \end{pmatrix}$, note $A\ne A^{T}$
1. $\lambda=1, -1$ are eigenvalues
2. take $\lambda=1$
	1. $\mathbf{x}=\begin{pmatrix} 1\\0 \end{pmatrix}, A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}$, so $\mathbf{x}$ is an eigenvector
3. take $\lambda=-1$
	1. $A+\mathbb{1}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix} 0&2\\0&0 \end{pmatrix}$, so $\mathbf{y}=\begin{pmatrix} 1\\-1 \end{pmatrix}$ is an eigenvector
4. clearly $\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}=\begin{pmatrix} 1\\0 \end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix} 1\\-1 \end{pmatrix}=0$
