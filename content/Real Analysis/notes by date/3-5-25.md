### Cauchy sequences
> [!definition]
> A sequence $( a_{n} )_{n\ge 1}$ in $\mathbb{R}$ is a **Cauchy sequence** if $\forall\varepsilon > 0$ $\exists n_{0}\in \mathbb{N}$ such that whenever $n, m\ge n_{0}$, $$\lvert a_{n}-a_{m} \rvert<\varepsilon.$$

> [!remark]
> Assume that $( a_{n} )_{n\ge 1}$ is Cauchy. 
> - Then $\forall\varepsilon>0$, $\exists n_{0}\in \mathbb{N}$ such that $\forall n, m\ge 0$, $\lvert a_{n}-a_{m} \rvert<\varepsilon$.
> - Fix $k\in \mathbb{N}$
> - Then this holds: $$\lim_{n \to \infty}a_{n+k}-a_{n}=0.$$
> - Why?
> 	- $\varepsilon>0$, so if $n\ge n_{0}$, $n+k\ge n_{0}$
> 	- Implying $\lvert a_{n+k}-a_{n} \rvert<\varepsilon$
> 	- ie. $\lim_{n \to \infty}a_{n+k}-a_{n}=0$
> ---
> The converse may not be true:
> - Assume that for every $k\in \mathbb{N}$, $\lim_{n \to \infty}a_{n+k}-a_{n}=0$
> - This doesn't imply $( a_{n} )$ is Cauchy.

> [!example]
> ##### $s_{n}=1+\frac{1}{2^{2}}+\frac{1}{3^{2}}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\ \ ... \text{is Cauchy.}$
> - Take $\lvert s_{n}-s_{m} \rvert$ where $n>m$
> - Then $\lvert s_{n}-s_{m} \rvert=\frac{1}{( m+1 )^{2}}+\frac{1}{( m+2 )^{2}}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n^{2}}$
> 	- So:
> 		$$
> 		\begin{align} \frac{1}{( m+k )^{2}}&\le \frac{1}{m+k-1}-\frac{1}{m+k}\\ &\le \frac{1}{( m+k-1 )( m+k )} \end{align}
> 		$$
> - So:
> 	$$
> 	\begin{align} \lvert s_{n}-s_{m} \rvert &\le\left(  \frac{1}{m}-\frac{1}{m+1}  \right)+\left(  \frac{1}{m+1}-\frac{1}{m+2}  \right)+\cdots+ \left(  \frac{1}{n-1}-\frac{1}{n}  \right) \\ &\le\frac{1}{m}-\frac{1}{n}\\&<\frac{1}{m}.\end{align}
> 	$$
> - Take $n_{0}$ such that $\frac{1}{n_{0}}<\varepsilon$
> - Therefore if $m,n\ge n_{0}$ then $\lvert s_{n}-s_{m} \rvert<\frac{1}{m}<\frac{1}{n_{0}}<\varepsilon.$ **qed**

> [!theorem]
> ##### Every convergent sequence is Cauchy.

> [!proof]
> - Assume that $\lim_{n \to \infty}a_{n}=L$.
> - Then $\forall \varepsilon>0$ $\exists n_{0}\in \mathbb{N}$ such that if $k\ge n_{0}$, then $\lvert a_{k}-L \rvert<\varepsilon$.
> - Fix $m, n\ge n_{0}$
> - We know $\lvert a_{n}-a_{m} \rvert\le \lvert a_{n}-L \rvert+ \lvert a_{m}-L \rvert< \varepsilon$
> 	- where $\frac{\varepsilon}{2}<\lvert a_{n}-L \rvert$, $\frac{\varepsilon}{2}<\lvert a_{m}-L \rvert$. **qed**

> [!theorem]
> ##### Every Cauchy sequence is bounded.

> [!proof]
> - Take $\varepsilon=1$
> - Then $\exists n_{0}$ such that for $n,m \ge n_{0}$, $\lvert a_{n}-a_{m} \rvert<1$
> - In particular, $\lvert a_{n}-a_{n_{0}}<1 \rvert$ for all $n\ge n_{0}$
> 	- So $\lvert a_{n} \rvert\le \lvert a_{n_{0}} \rvert+1$ for all $n\ge n_{0}$
> - Let $M=\text{max}\left\{ \lvert a_{1} \rvert, \lvert a_{2} \rvert, \cdots, \lvert a_{n_{0}-1} \rvert, \lvert a_{n_{0}} \rvert +1\right\}$
> - Then $\lvert a_{n} \rvert\le M$ for all $n\le 1$. **qed**

> [!theorem]
> ##### If $( a_{n} )$ is a Cauchy sequence that has a convergent subsequence, then it is convergent.

> [!proof]
> - $( a_{n} )$ is Cauchy
> 	- So $\forall\varepsilon>0 \exists N, \forall n,m\ge N, \lvert a_{n}-a_{m} \rvert<\varepsilon$
> - $( a_{n_j} )$ is convergent
> 	- $\longleftrightarrow \lim_{j \to 0}a_{n_{j}}=L$, 
> 		- $\exists k_{0}$ s.t. if $j\ge k_{0}$ then $\lvert a_{n_j} -L \rvert<\varepsilon$
> 	- $\longrightarrow\lvert a_{n}-L \rvert\le \lvert a_{n}-a_{n_{j}} \rvert+\lvert a_{n_{j}} -L \rvert$
> 	- so $n_{j}\ge N$, $j\ge k_{0}$
> 		- implies $M=\text{max}\left\{ N, n_{k_{0}} \right\}$
> 	- thus: $$n\ge M \longrightarrow\lvert a_{n}-L \rvert\le \lvert a_{n}-a_{n_{k_{0}}} \rvert+\lvert a_{n_{j}}-L \rvert.$$... **qed**.
##### A sequence in $\mathbb{R}$ is Cauchy *if and only if* it is convergent.
