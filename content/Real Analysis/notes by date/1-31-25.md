### Least Upper Bound Property (cont)
> [!definition]
> Let $A$ be a subset of $\mathbb{R}$. $A$ is **bounded above** if there exists $\beta \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $x\leq \beta$ for every $x\in A$.
> In this case, $\beta$ is called an **upper bound** for $A$.
> [!example]
> $$A=\left\{ 1-\frac{1}{n}, n\ge 1 \right\}=\left\{ 0, \frac{1}{2}, \frac{2}{3}, \cdots \right\}\ \forall  x\in  A,\ x\le 1$$
> - $1$ is an upper bound for $A$
> - $2$ is an  upper bound for $A$
> - $A$ does not have a maximum:
> 	- There is no number $x_{0}$ in $A$ such that $x\le x_{0}$ in $A$ for all $x\in A$
> [!remark]
> - *Bounded below* and *lower bound* are equivalent expressions
> - ==Small definition==: If $A$ is both bounded above and bounded below, we simply state that $A$ is *bounded*.
> [!definition]
> Let $E$ be a non-empty subset of $\mathbb{R}$. Given some $x\in \mathbb{R}$, we call $x$ a **least upper bound** (supremum of $E$) if the following hold:
> 1. $x$ is an upper bound of $E$
> 2. $x$ is the smallest such number in $E$ that is an upper bound
> 3. If $\beta \in \mathbb{R}$ satisfies $\beta < x$, then $\beta$ *is not* an upper bound of $E$.
> 4. ==Notation==: $x=\text{sup }E$
> [!remark]
> - Every non-empty subset of $\mathbb{R}$ that is bounded above *must have a supremum.*
> - Conversely, every non-empty subset of $\mathbb{R}$ that is bounded below *must have an infimum.*
> [!corollary]
> 5. If $E$ is *not* bounded above, then $\text{sup }E=\infty$
> 6. If $E$ is *not* bounded below, then $\text{inf }E=-\infty$
#### Consequences of the least upper bound property
- We can define the **Archimedean Property**:
> [!definition]
> If $x,y\in \mathbb{R}$ and $x>0$, then there exists $n \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $nx>y$.
> [!proof]
> 7. Assume $y>0$; then: $$A=\left\{ nx: n\in  \mathbb{N} \right\}$$
> 8. *Contradiction.* Assume that the property is not true; then: $$\forall n\in  \mathbb{N},\ nx\leq y$$... where $y$ is an upper bound of $A$.
> 	1. Let $x=\text{sup }A$
> 	2. Then $x-x$ is not an upper bound of $A$:
> 		1. We know for any $\epsilon>0$ there must be an element in $A$ greater than $x-\epsilon$
> 		2. Let $x=\epsilon$
> 		3. Let $n_{0}\in \mathbb{N}$ and $\alpha=\text{sup }A$
> 		4. $n_{0}x> x-x$
> 		5. $( n_{1}+1 )x>\alpha$
> 	4. but $( n_{0}+1 )x\in A$, *a contradiction.* **qed.**
> [!theorem]
> Let $E$ be a non-empty subset of $\mathbb{R}$ that is bounded above and $x$ is an upper bound. We say that $x$ is a supremum *if and only if:* $$\forall\epsilon>0,\ \exists x\in E \text{ s.t. }x-\epsilon<x\le x$$
> [!theorem]
> If $x,y\in \mathbb{R}$ and $x<y$, then there exists $r\in \mathbb{Q}$ such that $x<r<y$.
> - "$\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$"
> [!proof]
> 9. Assume $y-x>0$
> 10. Then the [[Archimedean property]] implies that there exists $n\in \mathbb{N}$ such that $n( y-x )>1$.
> 11. Therefore, $ny>1+nx$.
> 12. Consider $\left\{ k\in \mathbb{N}: k>nx \right\}\subseteq \mathbb{N}$ (non-empty)
> 	1. Observe that it has a smallest element, which we'll call $m$:
> 	2. $\underline{m>nx}>m-1\Longrightarrow nx+1>m$
> 		1. $\frac{m}{n}>x$
> 	3. $m<nx+1<ny$
> 		1. $\frac{m}{n}<y$
> 	4. $\frac{m}{n}\in \mathbb{Q}$
> 	5. $x<\frac{m}{n}<y$
