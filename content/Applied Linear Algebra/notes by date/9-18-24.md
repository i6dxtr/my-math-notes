==2.4.5== $4x+3y-z=0$, Find basis for this subspace (A)
1. Choose vector satisfying equation and isolate variable:
	$4(-3)+3(4)+(0)=0$ when $z=0$, so $\mathbf{x}=\begin{pmatrix} -3\\4\\0 \end{pmatrix}$
2. Set $x=0$ to isolate another variable
	Setting $x=0$, then satisfy equation:
		$4(0)+3(1)-(3)=0$ when $x=0$, so $\mathbf{y}=\begin{pmatrix} 0\\1\\3 \end{pmatrix}$
So $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}$ are basis vectors for the subspace $4x+3y-z=0$

### QR algorithm
- It's worth the modest effort in changing a given basis into an orthonormal basis:
	- $\text{col}(A)\longrightarrow$ Find a basis: $\left\{ \mathbf{v}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix} 1\\1\\1 \end{pmatrix},\mathbf{v}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix} 1\\2\\-1 \end{pmatrix} \right\}$
	- Find an *orthonormal* basis for $\text{col}(A)$:
		- $\mathbf{u}_{1}=\frac{\mathbf{v}_{1}}{\lvert \lvert \mathbf{v}_{1} \rvert \rvert}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\begin{pmatrix} 1\\1\\1 \end{pmatrix}$
		- $\mathbf{u}_{2}=\mathbf{v}_{2}-(\mathbf{u}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{2})\mathbf{u}_{1}$
		- $\mathbf{u}_{2}=\frac{\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2}}{\lvert \lvert \overline{\mathbf{u}_{2}} \rvert \rvert}$
		- $\mathbf{u}_{1}\cdot \overline{\mathbf{u}}_2=\mathbf{u}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{2}-(\mathbf{u}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{2})\mathbf{u}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{u}_{1}$
		- ... $=\mathbf{u}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{2}-\mathbf{u}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{2}$
			- $\left\{ \mathbf{u}_{1}, \mathbf{u}_{2} \right\}$ is an orthonormal basis for $\text{col}(A)$
	- The generalization of this to any subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is the **QR algorithm**

> [!definition]
> A $n \times n$ matrix $Q$ is **orthogonal** if the columns of $Q$ form an *orthonormal basis* for $\mathbb{R}^{n}$
>
> How many orthogonal $n \times n$ matrices are here with all entries either $0$ or $1$?
> - Exactly a single $1$ in the first column, where all other entries are $0$
> 	- or really, anything with a magnitude of $1$, if speaking generally
> - Likewise for the second, the third... up to $n$
> - Order of columns do *not* matter, meaning several permutations, so at least $n!$
> - Entries can be $0, -1, 1$, so $2n \cdot 2(n-1) \cdot \ ...\ = n!\cdot 2^{n}$

> [!example]
> - $1 \times1,\ [1], [-1]$
> - $2 \times 2$:

> \cos \vartheta  & \sin \vartheta \\ \sin \vartheta & -\cos \vartheta
> \end{pmatrix}

> ==Question==: Is the product of orthogonal matrices an orthogonal matrix?
> Take:
> - $A$ is an $m \times n$ matrix
> 	$A^{T}A[i.j]$, where $A^T$ is $n \times n$ and $A$ is $m \times m$
> 	$\rightarrow A[:,i]\cdot A[:,j]$
> 	$A=\begin{pmatrix} 1&1&1\\a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}\\1&1&1 \end{pmatrix}$
> 	$A^{T}=\begin{pmatrix} -a_{1}-\\-a_{2}-\\-a_{3}- \end{pmatrix}$
> 	$A^TA=\begin{pmatrix} -a_{1}-\\-a_{2}-\\-a_{3}- \end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix} 1&1&1\\a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}\\1&1&1 \end{pmatrix}$
> 	$A^TA[1,2]=a_{1}\cdot a_{2}$
> - $AA^{T}$ records all possible dot products of the rows of $A$
> - $Q$ is orthogonal ($n \times n$) if the columns for an orthonormal basis $Q[:, i]\cdot Q[:, j]=\begin{array}{c|c}0&\text{if }i\neq j\\1&\text{if }i=j \end{array}$
> - So $Q^TQ$ is the identity matrix:
> 	- $Q^tQ=\prod_{n}$
> - Notice $Q^T=Q^{-1}$, so $QQ^{T}=\prod_{n}$; then $Q^{T}$ is also an orthogonal matrix.
> - Thus, the columns of $Q^{T}$ form an orthonormal basis, hence the rectangle (---------)
