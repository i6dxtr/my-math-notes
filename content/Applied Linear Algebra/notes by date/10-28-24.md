## Eigenvalues & Vectors
     Recall definition of eigenvalues:
- $A$ is $n \times n$ and $\lambda\in \mathbb{R}$ is an **eigenvalue** if there is a *non-zero* vector $\mathbf{x}\in \mathbb{R}$ such that $A\mathbf{x}=\lambda \mathbf{x}$
#### Locating eigenvalues is different than solving a system of linear equations
- Why?
	- Let $\begin{pmatrix} 4&5\\6&7 \end{pmatrix}$. Solve for $\begin{pmatrix} x_{1}\\x_{2} \end{pmatrix}\neq \begin{pmatrix} 0\\0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$
	- $\begin{pmatrix} 4&5\\6&7 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x_{1}\\x_{2} \end{pmatrix}=\lambda\begin{pmatrix} x_{1}\\x_{2} \end{pmatrix}$
		- $4x_{i}+5x_{2}=\lambda x_{1}$
		- $6x_{1}+7x_{2}=\lambda x_{2}$
		- This problem is non-linear...
### How do we locate eigenvalue/vector pairs?
     Let $A=\begin{pmatrix} 4&-5\\1&2 \end{pmatrix}$. Find eigenvalues & bases for corresponding eigenspaces.
#### Find eigenvalues
  - ==recall==. $A\mathbf{x}=\lambda \mathbf{x} \leftrightarrow A\mathbf{x}-\lambda x=\mathbf{0}$
	  - $\leftrightarrow (A-\lambda\mathbb{1}_{2})\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$
	  - $\leftrightarrow A-\lambda\mathbb{1}_{2}$ is *not* invertible
	  - $\leftrightarrow \text{det}(A-\lambda\mathbb{1}_{2})=0$
  - This calculates $\lambda$, then finding vectors requires solving the system of linear equations

- *ex.* $\text{det}\left( \begin{pmatrix} 4&-5 \\ 1&-2 \end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix} \lambda&0\\0&\lambda \end{pmatrix} \right)=\text{det}\left( \begin{pmatrix} 4-\lambda & -5\\ 1& 2-\lambda \end{pmatrix} \right)=(4-\lambda)(-2-\lambda)+5$
	- $= -8-2\lambda+\lambda^{2}+5$
	- $=\lambda^{2}-2\lambda-3$
	- $=(\lambda-3)(\lambda+1)$
	- $=0.$
	- ... $\lambda=3,-1$ are the eigenvalues of $A$.
#### Now find eigenvectors
     $\lambda = 3$: Solve $A\mathbf{x}=3\mathbf{x}$
- $(A-3\mathbb{1}_{2})\mathbf{x}=0$
- $A-3\mathbb{1}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix} 1&-1\\1&-5 \end{pmatrix}\longrightarrow\begin{pmatrix} 1&-5\\0&0 \end{pmatrix}$
	- Let $x_{2}$ be free
	- $x_{1}=5x_{2}$
- So $\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 5\\1 \end{pmatrix} \right\}$ is a basis for $E_{3}$
     $\lambda=-1$: ...
- $A+\mathbb{1}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix} 5&-5\\1&-1 \end{pmatrix}\longrightarrow\begin{pmatrix} 1&-1\\0&0 \end{pmatrix}$
	- $x_{2}$ is free
	- $x_{1}=x_{2}$
- So $\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1\\1 \end{pmatrix} \right\}$ is a basis

*ex.* $\begin{pmatrix} 0&1\\-1&0 \end{pmatrix}$ has no (real) eigenvalues
- $(\lambda^{2}+1=0$ has no real solution)

> [!definition]
> A matrix $A$ is **positive (semi)definite**, P(S)D, if the following are true:
> 1. $A\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{x}\geq0\ \ \forall \mathbf{x}\in \mathbb{R}^{n}$
> 2. $A\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{x}\geq 0$  $\forall \mathbf{x}\in \mathbb{R}^{n}$

  *ex.* Let $A$ be an $n \times k$ matrix. Then $A^{T}A$ is PSD.
  - Why?
	  - $A^{T}A\mathbf{x}\cdot \mathbf{x}=A\mathbf{x}\cdot A\mathbf{x}=\lvert\lvert A\mathbf{x} \rvert\rvert^{2}\ge 0$

### Eigenvalues of PSD matrices
   If $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of (PSD) $A$, then $\lambda \geq 0$
- Why?
	- Suppose $A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}$.
	- Then $\lvert\lvert A\mathbf{x} \rvert\rvert^{2}=A\mathbf{x}\cdot\lambda\mathbf{x}=\lambda A\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{x}$
	- $\lambda=\frac{\lvert\lvert A\mathbf{x} \rvert\rvert^{2}}{A\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{x}}>0$
##### Projection matrices
   $P=P^{2}=P^{T}P$, so $P$ is PSD
   - $P$ is PSD $\longleftrightarrow$ $P=\mathbb{1}_{n}$
   We can say more in this case... $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue for $P$, then:
- $P_{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{x}}=(\lambda\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{x}=\lambda\lvert\lvert \mathbf{x} \rvert\rvert ^{2}$
- $P^{2}_{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{x}}=P\mathbf{x}\cdot P\mathbf{x}=\lambda^{2}\lvert\lvert \mathbf{x} \rvert\rvert^{2}$
- $\lambda=0,1$
##### Orthogonal matrices
*ex.* $Q$ is orthogonal and $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue. Then $\lambda=\pm 1$
- $Q\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}$, then...
- $\lvert\lvert \mathbf{x} \rvert\rvert^{2}=\lvert\lvert Q\mathbf{x} \rvert\rvert^{2}=Q\mathbf{x}\cdot Q\mathbf{x}=(\lambda \mathbf{x})\cdot(\lambda \mathbf{x})=\lambda ^{2}\lvert\lvert \mathbf{x} \rvert\rvert^{2}$

### Fundamental theorem of applied linear algebra

> [!theorem]
> Every symmetric matrix has a *real* eigenvalue
