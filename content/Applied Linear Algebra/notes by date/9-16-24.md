### Chapter 4
- ==recall==: $A\mathbf{x}\cdot \mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}\cdot A^T\mathbf{y}$
	- $A$ is $k \times m$
	- $\mathbf{x}$ is $m \times 1$
	- $\mathbf{y}$ is $k \times 1$
	- $Ax\cdot y$ same as $y^{T}(Ax)$
- ==recall==: two vectors $x,y\in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ are *orthogonal* (normal, perp.) if $x\cdot y=0$

> [!definition]
> If $V\subset \mathbb{R}^{n}$ s.t. $V$ is a subspace, we define the *perpendicular*: $$V^{\perp}=\left\{ x\in \mathbb{R}^{n}\ |\ x\cdot v=0 \ \ \forall \mathbf{v}\in  V\right\}$$

> [!remark]
> 1. $V\cap V^{\perp}=\mathbf{0}$
> 	1. completely orthogonal
> 2. $\text{span}\left\{ V, V^{\perp} \right\}=\mathbb{R}^{n}$
> 	1. span defined
> 3. $\text{dim}(V)+\text{dim}(V^{\perp})= n$

> [!corollary]
> $$ 
>  \text{null}(A^{T})=\text{col}(A)^{\perp}
> $$
>  Why? :
>  $$
>  >  \begin{align}
>  \mathbf{x}\in \text{null}(A^{T})&\longleftrightarrow A^{T}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}  \\
>  &\longleftrightarrow A^{T}\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}=0&& &\text{for all }y\in \mathbb{R}^{m} \\
>  &\longleftrightarrow \mathbf{x}\cdot A\mathbf{y}=0 && &\text{for all }y\in \mathbb{R}^{m} \\
>  &\longleftrightarrow \text{x is orthogonal  to every vector} \\
>  & \text{          in col}(A)^{\perp} \\
>  &\longleftrightarrow x\in  \text{col}(A)^{\perp} \\
>  \text{col}(A)&=\left\{ A\mathbf{y}\ :\ y\in  \mathbb{R}^{m} \right\} \\
>  &= \text{span}\left\{ \text{columns of }A \right\}
>  \end{align}
>
>  $$

- $A^{T}$ is $m \times k$
- $\text{col}(A)\subset \mathbb{R}^{k}\longrightarrow \text{dim}(\text{col}(A)^{\perp})=k-\text{rank}(A)$
	- $\text{dim}(\text{col}(A))+\text{dim}(\text{col}(A)^{\perp})=k$
- $k=\text{rank}(A^{T})+\text{nullity}(A^{T})$
   $=\text{rank}(A^{T})+k-\text{rank}(A)$
   $\rightarrow \text{rank}(A)=\text{rank}(A^{T})$
#### Orthogonality & why it's useful

> [!definition]
> A set $\left\{ u_{1}, ..., u_{k} \right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is **orthogonal** if $\mathbf{u}_{1}\ ...\ \mathbf{u}_{j}=\mathbf{0}$ when $i\neq j$. 
> - Conversely, the set is *orthonormal* if $\mathbf{u}_1\ ...\ \mathbf{u}_j=\Bigg\lvert\begin{array}{c}0 &&\text{if }i\neq j\\ 1&&\text{if }i=j \end{array}$

- Any orthogonal set can be normalized to an orthogonal $\text{ref}$
1. $\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1\\1 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 1\\-1 \end{pmatrix} \right\}$ is *orthogonal*, but not orthonormal
   $\begin{pmatrix} 1\\1 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 1\\-1 \end{pmatrix}=1-1=0$

2. $\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\\\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\\\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\0\\-\frac{1}{2}\\  \end{pmatrix} \right\}$
	1. $\mathbf{u}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{u}_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}+0-\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}=0$
	2. $\lvert \lvert \mathbf{u}_{1} \rvert \rvert^{2}=\mathbf{u}_{1}+\mathbf{u}_{1}=\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{3}$
	3. $\lvert \lvert \mathbf{u}_{2} \rvert \rvert^{2}=\mathbf{u}_{2}\cdot \mathbf{u}_{2}=\frac{1}{2}+0+\frac{1}{2}=1$
3. means: $\mathbf{u}_{1}=\frac{\mathbf{v}_{1}}{\lvert \lvert \mathbf{v}_{1} \rvert \rvert}=\begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\\\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \end{pmatrix}$, $\mathbf{u}_{2}=...=\begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\\\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \end{pmatrix}$
Orthogonality is "super linear independence"
- ==Fact==: Every orthogonal set is linearly independent
	$\mathbf{v}_{1}, \mathbf{v}_{2}, \mathbf{v}_{3}$ is orthogonal, check linear independence:
	Suppose $c_{1}v_{1}+c_{2}v_{2}+c_{3}v_{3}=\mathbf{0}$. 
	==Claim==: $c_{1}=c_{2}=c_{3}=0$.
	==Idea==: Dot Product both sides with $\mathbf{v}_{1}$:
	$$
	\begin{align}
	\mathbf{v}_{1}=(c_{1}\mathbf{v}_{1}+c_{2}\mathbf{v}_{2}+c_{3}\mathbf{v}_{3})&=\mathbf{v}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{0}&=0 \\
	&=c_{1}\mathbf{v}_{1}\cdot \mathbf{v}_{1}+c_{2}\mathbf{v}_{2}\cdot \mathbf{v}_{1}+c_{3}\mathbf{v}_{3}\cdot \mathbf{v}_{1}&=0 \\
	&= c_{1}\lvert \lvert \mathbf{v}_{1} \rvert \rvert^{2}&=0
	\end{align}
	$$
    Since $\mathbf{v}_{1}\neq \mathbf{0}$, we have $c_{1}=0$.
- ==Fact==: Orthogonality saves computing time.
	- $\left\{ \mathbf{b}_{1}, ..., \mathbf{b}_{n} \right\}$ is a basis for $\mathbb{R}^{n}$
	- on the other hand, $\left\{ \mathbf{u}_{1}, ..., \mathbf{u}_{n} \right\}$ is an orthogonal basis for $V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}$, so...
		- $x=\beta_{1}u_{1}+...+\beta_{n}u_{n}$
		- $y=\mu_{1}u_{1}+...+\mu_{n}u_{n}$
		- $x\cdot y=(\beta_{1}u_{1}+...+\beta_{n}u_{n})\cdot(\mu_{1}u_{1}+...+\mu_{n}u_{n})$
		- ... $n$ computations
- $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}\in V$
	- $\mathbf{x}=\alpha_{1}\mathbf{b}_{1}+\alpha_{2}\mathbf{b}_{2}+...+\alpha_{n}\mathbf{b}_{n}$
	- $\mathbf{y}=\alpha_{1}\mathbf{b}_{1}+\alpha_{2}\mathbf{b}_{2}+...+\alpha_{n}\mathbf{b}_{n}$
	- $\mathbf{x}\cdot \mathbf{y}$... $n^{2}$ computations
- ==Fact==: Locating basis coefficients:
	- $\mathbf{x}, \left\{ b_{1}, ..., b_{n} \right\}$ basis for $\mathbf{x}\in V\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}$
	- There are $\alpha_{1}, ..., \alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{R}$ so $\mathbf{x}=\alpha_{1}b_{1}+...+\alpha _{n} b_{n}$:
		$$
		\begin{align*}
		\mathbf{b}_{1}&=\begin{pmatrix} 1\\1 \end{pmatrix},\ \  \mathbf{b}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix} -2\\3 \end{pmatrix}, \ \ \mathbf{x}=\begin{pmatrix} 1\\7 \end{pmatrix} \\
		&&&\text{Write }\begin{pmatrix} 1\\7 \end{pmatrix}=\alpha_{1}\begin{pmatrix} 1\\1 \end{pmatrix}+\alpha_{2}\begin{pmatrix} -2\\3 \end{pmatrix} \\ \\
		&
		\left(\begin{array}{cc|c}
		1 & -2 & 1 \\
		1 & 3 & 7
		\end{array}\right) \\
		&&&\text{ Solve for }\beta\text{ by calculating dot prod.} \\ &&&\text{and NOT solving a system of eqns} \\
		\mathbf{u}_{1}\cdot \mathbf{x}&=u_{1}\cdot(\beta_{1}u_{1}+...+\beta_{n}u_{n}) \\
		&=\beta_{1}u_{1}\cdot u_{1}+ ...+ \beta_{n}u_{n}\cdot u_{n} \\
		\mathbf{x}&=\frac{u_{1}\cdot\mathbf{x}}{\lvert \lvert \mathbf{u}_{1} \rvert  \rvert ^{2}} .........
		\end{align*}
		$$
