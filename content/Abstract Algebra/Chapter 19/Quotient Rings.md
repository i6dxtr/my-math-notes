> [!definition]
> Let $A$ be a [[Rings|ring]] and $J$ an [[ideal]] of $A$. We define their **quotient ring** as: $$A / J=\left\{ J+x\ |\ x\in  A \right\}$$... which is a ring of [[Quotient Groups|quotient groups]] with the following operations:
> 1. Addition: $( J+x )+( J+y )=J+( x+y )$
> 2. Multiplication: $( J+x )( J+y )=J+xy$

> [!remark]
> Since $( A,+ )$ is an [[abelian group]] and $J$ is a [[normal subgroup]], addition of cosets is well defined; moreover, $A / J$, with addition of cosets, forms an abelian group itself.

- $J+x$ is called a *coset* of $J$ in $A$.

> [!proof] Proof: $A / J$ is a ring.
> ##### Proving coset multiplication is well-defined
> ###### If $J + x = J + x'$ and $J + y = J + y'$, then $J + xy = J + x'y'$.
> 1) First, since $x$ and $x'$ are in the same coset:
> 	- $J + x = J + x'$ implies $x - x' \in J$ 
> 	- Let $a = x - x'$
> 		- so $x = x' + a$ where $a \in J$
> 1) Similarly for $y$ and $y'$:
> 	- $J + y = J + y'$ implies $y - y' \in J$ 
> 	- Let $b = y - y'$, 
> 		- so $y = y' + b$ where $b \in J$ 
> 1) Now let's multiply $x$ and $y$:
> 	- $xy = (x' + a)(y' + b)$ $= x'y' + x'b + ay' + ab$
> 2) Rearrange: $xy = x'y' + (x'b + ay' + ab)$
> 3) Key properties of ideal $J$:
> 	- $ab \in J$ (closed under multiplication) 
> 	- $x'b \in J$ (ideal absorbs products) 
> 	- $ay' \in J$ (ideal absorbs products) 
> 	- Sum of elements in $J$ is in $J$ (closed under addition) 
> 1) Therefore:
> 	- $(x'b + ay' + ab) \in J$ 
> 	- $xy - x'y' \in J$ 
> 	- So $J + xy = J + x'y'$.
>
> Thus, multiplication of cosets is well-defined.

> [!example]
> ##### $\mathbb{Z} / \left\langle n \right\rangle=\left\{ \left\langle n \right\rangle+0, \left\langle n \right\rangle+1,...,\left\langle n \right\rangle+( n-1 ) \right\}$
> 1. for $n\in \mathbb{Z}^{+}$
> 	1. $\mathbb{Z} / \left\langle n \right\rangle\cong \mathbb{Z}_{n}$
> 	2. This can be observed by applying the [[Fundamental Homomorphism Theorem]] to the function $f:\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{n}$ defined as $f( k )=k\text{ mod }n$

### Connections between types of ideals and quotient rings

> [!theorem]
> Let $A$ be a commutative [[Rings|ring]] with [[unity]] and let $K$ be an [[ideal]] of $A$.
> 1. $A / K$ is a commutative ring with unity.
> 2. If $K$ is a [[(semi)prime ideals|prime ideal]], then $A / K$ is an [[integral domain]].
> 3. If $K$ is a [[maximal ideal]], then $A / K$ is a [[field]].

> [!proof]
> ##### Proof of 1.
> 1. Let $K+x, K+y\in A /K$ $( x,y\in A )$.
> 	1. so $( K+x )( K+y )=K+xy$
> 		1. $=K+yx$
> 		2. $=( K+y )( K+x )$
> 	2. ... so $A / K$ is commutative.
> 2. *Claim:* $K+1$ is a multiplicative identity element for $A / K$
> 	1. $( K+x )( K+1 )=K+x1=K+x$
>
> ##### Proof of 2.
> 1. Suppose $K$ is prime    *...i.e. $ab\in K\longrightarrow a\in k$ or $b\in K$*
> 2. We'll show $A / K$ is an integral domain
> 	1. Sufficient to show there are no zero divisors
> 3. Suppose toward a contradiction, $K+ x, K+ y\in A / K$ and zero divisors (i.e. $x,y\in A$)
> 	1. $K+x\ne K+0$
> 		1. $K+0$ is the zero element of $A / K$
> 	2. $K+y\ne K+0$
> 	3. $( K+x )( K+y )=K+0$
> 4. Then $K+xy=K+0$
> 	1. so $xy\in K+0=K$
> 5. $K$ is prime, so $x\in K$ or $y\in K$
> 	1. so $x\in K$ or $y\in K$
> 	2. so $x\in K+0$ or $y\in K+0$
> 	3. so $K+x=K+0$ or $K+y=K+0$, a contradiction
> 		1. recall $a\in K+b\equiv K+a=K+b$
>
> ##### Proof of 3.
> 1. Suppose $K$ is maximal    ...*i.e. no ideal $I$ s.t. $K\subset I\subset A$*
> 2. Let $K+x\in A / K$ be nonzero   *....i.e.* $K+x\ne K+0$
> 3. We'll show it has an inverse in $A / K$
> 	1. Then $x\not\in K+0$ ...*i.e.* $x\notin K$
> 4. *Idea:* Let $I$ be the ideal generated by $K$ and $x$
> 	1. *i.e. the smallest subset with respect to the ideal containing $K$ and $x$ (by maximality of $K$, we then have $I=A$)*
> 	2. Define $I=\left\{ k+ax\ |\ k\in K\text{ and }a\in A \right\}$
> 	3. Check that $I$ is an ideal:
> 		1. Let $k+ax, k'+a'x\in I$
> 		2. So $( k+ax )+( k'+a'x )=( k+k' )+( a+a' )x\in I$
> 			1. $( k+k' )\in K$, $( a+a' )\in A$
> 		3. $0=0+0x\in I$
> 			1. $0\in K$
> 			2. $0x\in A$
> 		4. $-( k+ax )=kk'+ka'x+axk'+axa'x$
> 			1. $=( kk'+ka'x+axk' )+( axa' )x\in I$
> 				1. $( kk'+ka'x+axk' )\in K$
> 				2. $( axa' )\in A$
> 	4. $I$ is an ideal containing $K$ and $x$
> 	5. So $K\subset I$
> 	6. By maximality of $K$, we then have $I=A$
> 	7. Since $I=A, 1\in I$
> 	8. so $1=k+ax$ for some $k\in K$ and $a\in A$
> 	9. $( K+x )( K+a )=K+ax=K+1$    *... where $K+1$ the identity of $A / K$*
> 		1. since $ax\in K+1$, which is b/c $ax=-k+1$
> 			1. ... where $-k\in K$
> 	10. Similarly, $( K+x )( K+a )=K+1$
> 	11. So $K+x$ has an inverse $( K+a )$
