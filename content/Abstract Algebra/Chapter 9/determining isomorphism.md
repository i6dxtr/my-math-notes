> [!remark] Determing if two groups are isomorphic
> We must *find* an [[isomorphism]] from $G_{1}$ to $G_{2}$ in a sequential manner:
> 1. Make an educated guess, and come up with a function $f:G_{1}\rightarrow G_{2}$ which looks as though it might be an isomorphism
> 2. Check that $f$ is [[bijective]]
> 3. Check that $f$ satisfies the identity $f(ab)=f(a)f(b)$

> [!example]
> - Take $\mathbb{R}$ (closed under *addition*) and $\mathbb{R}^{+}$ (closed under *multiplication*)
> - Determine their isomorphism:
> 	- Take $f(x)=e^{x}$ to be the function from $\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}$
> 	- $f$ is injective: if $y\in \mathbb{R}^{+}$ then $y=e^{\text{ln }y}=f(\text{ln }y)$
> 	- $f(ab)=f(a)f(b)$ since $e^{a+b}=e^{a}+e^{b}$
> Therefore, $\mathbb{R}$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^{+}$ i.e. $\mathbb{R}\cong \mathbb{R}^{+}$

> [!remark] Determining non-isomorphism
> Determining non-isomorphism between $G_{1}$ and $G_{2}$ (not comprehensive):
> 1. $G_{1}$ is *commutative* and $G_{2}$ is *not*.
> 2. $G_{1}$ has element that's its own inverse, and the opposite for $G_{2}$
> 3. $G_{1}$ is generated by two elements, while $G_{2}$ is not. (aka, one is cyclic and the other is not)
> 4. All elements in $G_{1}$ is the square of some element in itself, while this isn't the case for $G_{2}$ 
> 5. (trivial) There exists no bijection from $G_{1}$ and $G_{2}$
