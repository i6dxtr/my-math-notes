## Isomorphism
- meaning, "same structure" in an abstract sense
- think of palindromes
- example: flow networks
	- set of points w/ arrows joining some
	- represents channels of communication, electric circuits, etc. ![[Pasted image 20241014134439.png]]
	- creating a superposition of the points in $(A)$ to points in $(B)$ shows the two are *isomorphic*:
		$$
		\begin{pmatrix}
		1&2&3&4 \\ 6&5&8&7
		\end{pmatrix}
		$$
		... which retains the direction of flow between points.
	- we call this an **isomorphism** from network $(A)$ to $(B)$
Consider the following groups:
![[Pasted image 20241014135012.png]]
- $G_{1}, G_{2}$ clearly different, but they are still isomorphic, since replacing elements in $G_{1}$ with those in $G_{2}$, we can see that $G_{1}$ *coincides* with $G_{2}$ forming a one-to-one correspondence in the following fashion: $$\begin{pmatrix} 0&1&2 \\ e&a&b \end{pmatrix}$$ ... essentially transforming $G_{1}$ to $G_{2}$
Generally speaking, if there exists an isomorphism between two groups, there is a one-to-one correspondence between them. Formally: $$f(a)=a'\wedge f(b)=b' \longrightarrow f(ab)=a'b'$$![[Pasted image 20241014140110.png]]... from which we derive the definition, in more concise terms:

> [!definition]
> Let $G_{1}$ and $G_{2}$ be groups. A [[bijective]] function $f:G_{1}\rightarrow G_{2}$ with the property that for any two elements $a,b\in G_{1}$: $$f(ab)=f(a)f(b)$$ ... is called an **isomorphism** from $G_{1}$ to $G_{2}$. If there exists an isomorphism from $G_{1}$ to $G_{2}$, we say that $G_{1}$ is **isomorphic** to $G_{2}$, or: $$G_{1}\cong G_{2}$$

### Determining Isomorphism
We must *find* an isomorphism from $G_{1}$ to $G_{2}$ in a sequential manner:
1. Make an educated guess, and come up with a function $f:G_{1}\rightarrow G_{2}$ which looks as though it might be an isomorphism
2. Check that $f$ is bijective
3. Check that $f$ satisfies the identity $f(ab)=f(a)f(b)$

> [!example]
> - Take $\mathbb{R}$ (closed under *addition*) and $\mathbb{R}^{+}$ (closed under *multiplication*)
> - Determine their isomorphism:
> 	- Take $f(x)=e^{x}$ to be the function from $\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}$
> 	- $f$ is injective: if $y\in \mathbb{R}^{+}$ then $y=e^{\text{ln }y}=f(\text{ln }y)$
> 	- $f(ab)=f(a)f(b)$ since $e^{a+b}=e^{a}+e^{b}$
> Therefore, $\mathbb{R}$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^{+}$ i.e. $\mathbb{R}\cong \mathbb{R}^{+}$

Determining non-isomorphism between $G_{1}$ and $G_{2}$ (not comprehensive):
1. $G_{1}$ is *commutative* and $G_{2}$ is *not*.
2. $G_{1}$ has element that's its own inverse, and the opposite for $G_{2}$
3. $G_{1}$ is generated by two elements, while $G_{2}$ is not.
4. All elements in $G_{1}$ is the square of some element in itself, while this isn't the case for $G_{2}$ 
5. (trivial) There exists no bijection form $G_{1}$ and $G_{2}$
