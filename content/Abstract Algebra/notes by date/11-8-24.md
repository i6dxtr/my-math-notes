## Chapter 17: Rings

> [!definition]
> A **ring** is some set $A$ with two (abstract) operations, $+$ and $\cdot$, albeit with the caveat that they are somehow analogous to the standard definition of addition and multiplication, such that:
> 1. $( A,+ )$ is an abelian group
> 2. $( A, \cdot )$ is associative
> 3. $\cdot$ distributes over addition: $$a\cdot( b+c )=a\cdot b+a\cdot c\text{ ... and ...  }( b+c )\cdot a=b\cdot a+c \cdot a$$

> [!example]
> 1. $\mathbb{Z}$ under ordinary addition, multiplication
> 2. $\mathbb{Q}$, same as above
> 3. $\mathbb{R}$, same as above
> 4. $M_{n}( \mathbb{R} )$ with matrix addition and multiplication
> 5. $\mathbb{Z}_{n}$ with addition/multiplication $\text{mod }n$
> 6. $\mathcal{P}( S )$ with $X+X=( X / Y )\cup ( Y / X )$ and $XY=X\cap Y$
> 	1. We know $( \mathcal{P}( S ),+ )$ is an abelian group
> 	2. $\cap$ is associative

> 		2. $x\cap( y\cap z )$[[Drawing 2024-11-08 15.08.18.excalidraw|fig]]
> 		3. $X\cap( Y+z )$[[Drawing 2024-11-08 15.09.27.excalidraw|fig]]
> 			1. $Y+Z$[[Drawing 2024-11-08 15.09.56.excalidraw|fig]]
> 		4. $( X\cap Y )+( X\cap Z )$[[Drawing 2024-11-08 15.10.54.excalidraw|fig]]

#### Subtraction
Let $A$ with $+ ,\cdot$ be a ring, i.e.,
1. $( A,+ )$ is an abelian group
2. $\cdot$ is associative
3. $\cdot$ distributes over $+$
Then, *subtraction* is defined by $a-b=a+( -b )$

#### Ring axioms
> [!remark]
> ##### In any ring, the following must hold:
> 1. $a\cdot0=0$
> 2. $a\cdot( -b )=-( a\cdot b )$
> 3. $( -a )\cdot( -b )=a\cdot b$

> [!proof]
> ##### Proving 1.)
> 1. WTS $a\cdot0$ satisfies the definition of an identity element
> 	1. Cancellation law: $a+b=a+c\longrightarrow b=c$
> 	2. Take $a\cdot a+0=a\cdot a$ ($0$ is the identity for $+$)
> 		1. $=a\cdot ( a+0 )$
> 		2. $=a\cdot a+ a\cdot 0$ (distribution)
> 		3. $=a\cdot 0$ (cancellation)
> ##### Proving 2.)
> 1. WTS $a\cdot( -b )$ and $a\cdot b$ are additive inverses
> 	1. $a\cdot (  -b )+ a \cdot b$
> 		1. $=a \cdot (  ( -b ) + b )$ (distribution)
> 		2. $= a \cdot 0$ (definition of subtraction)
> 		3. $= 0.$ (by $1.$)

#### Unity

#### Trivial ring
The ring $A=\left\{ 0 \right\}$ is called the *trivial ring*. Technically, it is a ring with unity, since $0$ satisfies the definition of multiple identity.

> [!remark]
> In a nontrivial ring with unity, $0\ne 1$

> [!proof]
> 1. Suppose $0=1$.
> 2. WTS the ring $A$ is trivial
> 	1. Let $a\in A$. 
> 	2. WTS $a=0$
> 		1. $a \cdot 1=a \cdot 0$
> 		2. $a = 0$.
> 	3. So $a=0$
> 3. So $A$ is trivial.  *QED*

#### Fields

> [!definition]
> A **field** is a *nontrivial* commutative (under multiplicaton) ring with unity, in which every nonzero element has an inverse, i.e., a multiplicative inverse.
