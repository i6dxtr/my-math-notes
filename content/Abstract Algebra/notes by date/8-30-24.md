Additional example of operation:
*The group of integers modulo $n$*:
- defined as $\mathbb{Z}_{n}=\{ 0,1,2,...,n-1 \}$, $+_{n}$ is addition $\text{mod }n$
- eg. for $n=10$: 
	$4+_{10}9 =4+9\text{ mod }10$
	 $=13\text{ mod }10$
	 $=3$.
	- think of a clock
Note: $\mathbb{Z}_{n}$ is an example of a *finite* group, so we can make an operation table
- eg. for $n=5$
$$
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
\underline{t_{5}}  &  \underline{0} & \underline{1} & \underline{2} & \underline{3} & \underline{4} \\
0  & 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4  & \\
1 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4  & \underline{0}  & \text{: since }1+4\text{ mod }5=0\\
2 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 0 & 1 &  \\
3 & 3 & 4 & 0 & 1 & 2 &  \\
4 & 4 & 0 & 1 & 2 & \underline{3} & \text{: since }4+4\text{ mod }5=3
\end{array}
$$

**Abelian group**:
- a group whose operation is commutative $(x*y=y*x)$ (after Mels Abel)
	- ex. $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, $(\mathbb{Z}_{n}+_{n})$ are abelian, while $(GL_{n}(\mathbb{R}), \cdot)$ is not for $n\geq 2$
- note, in terms of table, commutativity means the bale is symmetric across the main diagonal.

**Another example of non-abelian group**
$SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})$= the set of all $n \times n$ matrices whose determinant is $1$.
Proof that this is a group under matrix multiplication:
1. Let $A,b\in SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})$. WTS $AB\in SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})$
	$\text{det}(AB)=\text{det}(A)\text{det}(B)=1\cdot1=1$
2. Matrix multiplication is associative in general
3. There is an identity element $I_{n}$
	$I_{n}\in SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})$ since $\text{det}(I_{n})=1$
4. Let $A\in SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})$. WTS $\underline{A}^{-1}\in SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})$ (ud: exists since $\text{det}(A)\neq0$)
	$\text{det}(A^{-1})=\frac{1}{\text{det}(A)}=\frac{1}{1}=1$

**More examples of group tables**
Let A be the following matrix:
$$
\begin{align}
A&=\begin{pmatrix}
0 & 1 \\
-1 & 0
\end{pmatrix} \\
A^{2}&=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & 1 \\
-1 & 0
\end{pmatrix}\cdot
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & 1 \\
0 & -1
\end{pmatrix}=
\begin{pmatrix}
-1 & 0 \\
0 & -1
\end{pmatrix}=-I \\
A^{3}&=-A=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & -1 \\
1 & 0
\end{pmatrix} \\
A^{4}&=I \\
G&=\{ I, A, -I, -A \} \\
\ &... \text{ is a group under matrix multiplication}.
\end{align}
$$
- Make the table:
$$
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
\ \  \cdot & \underline{I} & \underline{A} & \underline{-I} & \underline{A} \\
\ \ I & I & A & -I & -A \\
\ \ A & A & -I & -A & I \\
-I & -I & -A & I & A \\
-A & -A & I & A & -I
\end{array}
$$
- $G$ is *abelian*
- note how each diagonal is fully positive or negative
	- Same "pattern" as $\mathbb{Z}_{5}$

Ex.
Let $B=\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$; then $B^{2}=\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}=I$.
Look at $G=\{ I,B,-I, B \}$. This is a group under matrix multiplication:
$$
\begin{array}{c|cccc} 
  \underline{\cdot} & \underline{I} & \underline{B} & \underline{-I} & \underline{B} \\
I & I & B & -I & B \\
B & B & I & -B & I \\
-I & -I & -B & I & B \\
-B & -B & -I & B & I
\end{array}
$$
- Note the different pattern: long diagonals have $I$, $-B$

**Chapter 4: Elementary properties of groups**
In general we can write $a*b$ as $ab$ even if $*$ is not literally multiplication (multiplicative notation)
Theorem: (cancellation)
Let $a,b,c\in G$ where $G$ is a group.
1. If $ab=ac$ then $b=c$
2. If $ba=ca$ then $b=c$
Proof:
1. Suppose $ab=ac$. Then:
	$$
	\begin{align}
	a^{-1}ab&=a^{-1}ac \\
	eb&=ec \\
	b&=c.
	\end{align}
	$$
2. Suppose $ba=ca$. Then:
	$$
	\begin{align}
	baa^{-1}&=caa^{-1}  \\
	be&=ce \\
	b&=c.
	\end{align}
	$$
- note, we just appended $a^{-1}$ to both sides
- note, $ab=ca\not\rightarrow b=c$, in general
- a counter-example can be found in $GL_{2}(\mathbb{R})$

Theorem:
Let $a,b\in G$ where $G$ is a group. Then $(ab)^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1}$
Proof:
WTS: $ab$ and $b^{-1}a^{-1}$ are inverses of each other
$$
\begin{align}
abb^{-1}a^{-1}&=aea^{-1}=aa^{-1}&=e. \\
b^{-1}a^{-1}ab&=b^{-1}eb=b^{-1}b&=e.
\end{align}
$$
