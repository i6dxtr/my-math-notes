## Chapter 8: Permutations of a Finite Set

> [!definition]
> A $k-$cycle, written $(a_{1}, a_{2}, \cdots a_{k})$ for some distinct $a_{1}, ..., a_{k}\in \left\{ 1,...,n \right\}$ is the permutation $A_{1}|\rightarrow a_{2}$, $a_{2}|\rightarrow a_{3}$, ........, $a_{k-1}|\rightarrow a_{k}$, $a_{k}|\rightarrow a_{1}$ and $b|\rightarrow b$ for all other $b\in \left\{ 1,..,b \right\}$
>
> ex. in $S_{5}$
> $$
> (2\ 3\ 5)=
> \begin{pmatrix}
> 1  & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \\
> 1 & 3 & 5 & 4 & 2
> \end{pmatrix}
> $$
> - two cycles are *disjoint* if they do not involve any element in common
> 	- $(a_{1}\cdots a_{k})$ and $(b_{1}\cdots b_{k})$ are disjoint of $\left\{ a_{1},...,a_{k} \right\}\cap \left\{ b_{1},...,b_{k} \right\}\neq\emptyset$
>
> ex. in $S_{5}$
> - $(2\ 3\ 5)$ and $(1\ 4)$ are disjoint
>
> ==note==: disjoint cycles commute
> - eg. $(2\ 3\ 5)(1\ 4)=(1\ 4)(2\ 3\ 5)=\begin{pmatrix} 1&2&3&4&5\\4&3&5&1&2 \end{pmatrix}$

> [!theorem]
> Every element of $S_{n}$ (or more generally, $S_{A}$ where $A$ is a finite set) can be written as a product of some number of (pair-wise) disjoint cycles
> - allow trivial cases
> 	- the product of just 1 cycle $(a_{1}\ a_{2}\cdots a_{k})$ is itself, $(a_{1}\ a_{2}\cdots a_{k})$
> 	- the product of $0$ cycles (empty product) is $e$.
> ex.
> write $\begin{pmatrix} 1&2&3&4&5&6&7&8&9\\7&2&6&1&8&3&4&9&8 \end{pmatrix}$ as a product of disjoint cycles.
> soln. 
> - $\rightarrow1\rightarrow7\rightarrow4\rightarrow:$ $(1\ 7\ 4)$
> - $\rightarrow2\rightarrow:$ $(2)$
> - $\rightarrow3\rightarrow6\rightarrow:(3\ 6)$
> - $\rightarrow5\rightarrow8\rightarrow9\rightarrow:(5\ 8\ 9)$
> - = $(1\ 7\ 4)(3\ 6)(5\ 8\ 9)$
> ==note==: A "one-cycle", e.g. 2, is just $e$, so it can (and should) be omitted
> - this representation as a product of disjoint cycles is *unique* except for the order (since disjoint cycles commute)
> 	- $(1\ 7\ 4)=(7\ 4\ 1)=(4\ 1\ 7)$
>
> - by convention, we write a cycle with the smallest number first
>
> ex.
> Write every element of $D_{4}$ as a product of some number of disjoint cycles
> where 1,2 on right side of square:
> - $0^{\circ}\text{ rot}$: $e$
> - $90^{\circ}\text{ rot}$: $(1\ 2\ 3\ 4)$
> - $180^{\circ}\text{ rot}$: $(1\ 3)(2\ 4)$
> - $270^{\circ}\text{ rot}$: $(1\ 4\ 3\ 2)$
> - reflection across $y$: $(1\ 4)(2\ 3)$
> - reflection across $x=y$: $(2\ 4)$
> - reflection across $x$: $(1\ 2)(3\ 4)$
> - reflection across $-x=y$: $(1\ 3)$
>
> #### Multiplication (composition) in cycle notation
> continuing example
> Write $(1\ 2\ 3\ 4)(2\ 4)$ as a product of disjoint cycles
> - $\rightarrow1\rightarrow2\rightarrow:(1\ 2)$
> - $\rightarrow2\rightarrow3\rightarrow:(3\ 4)$
>
> A 2-cycle is called a **transposition**
> - let $\sigma\in S_{n}$
>   $\sigma^{2}=e$ if and only if $\sigma$ can be written as a product of disjoint transpositions. 
> pf. 
> Let $(a_{1}\ b_{1})(a_{2}\ b_{2}),...,(a_{i}\ b_{i})$ be disjoint transpositions. Then $$((a_{1}\ b_{1})\cdots(a_{i}\ b_{i}))^{2}=(a_{1}\ b_{1})^{2}\cdots(a_{i}\ b_{i})^{2}=e\cdots e=e$$
> - note that disjoint cycles commute and the square of a transposition is $e$

> [!theorem]
> Every element of $S_{n}$ can be written as a product of some number of transpositions, which are not necessarily disjoint.
> ==method==:
> 1. Write it as a product of disjoint cycles
> 2. Rewrite each cycle: $(a_{1}\ a_{2}\ \cdots a_{k})=(a_{1}\ a_{2})(a_{2}\ a_{3})\cdots(a_{k-1}\ a_{k})$.
> ex.
> $(1\ 2\ 3\ 4)=(1\ 2)(2\ 3)(3\ 4)$
> - see image
> ex.
> write $\begin{pmatrix} 1&2&3&4&5&6&7&8&9\\3&1&4&5&9&2&6&8&7 \end{pmatrix}$ as a product of transposes:
> soln: $\begin{pmatrix} 1&3&4&5&9&7&6&2 \end{pmatrix}=(1\ 3)(3\ 4)(4\ 5)(5\ 9)(9\ 7)(7\ 6)(6\ 2)$

> [!definition]
> An element of $S_{n}$ is called **even** if it can be written as the product of an even number of transpositions. The element is **odd** in the opposite case.

> [!theorem]
> Every element of $S_{n}$ is even or odd, but not both.

> [!definition]
> The **alternating group** on $n$ elements, $A_{n}$, is $$A_{n}=\left\{ \sigma\in  S_{n}\ |\ \sigma \text{ is even} \right\}$$ This is a group of $S_{n}$
