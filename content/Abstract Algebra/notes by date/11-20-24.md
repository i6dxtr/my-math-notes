> [!definition]
> For rings $A$ and $B$, $A$ is *isomorphic* to $B$, written $A\cong B$, means there is a function $f:A\rightarrow B$ that is an isomorphism, meaning:
> 1. $\forall x,y\in A$, $f( x+y )=f( x )+f( y )$
> 2. $\forall x,y\in A$, $f( xy )=f( x )f( y )$
> 3. $f$ is bijective

> [!remark]
> If $A\cong B$ and $A$ is a field, then $B$ is also a field. Moreover, if $A$ is an ID (?) then so is $B$.

> 1. Recall definition of [[ideal]] (moreover, the *principal ideal*)

> [!definition]
> Let $A$ be a commutative ring and $a\in A$. The **principal ideal** generated by $a$ is: $$\left\langle a \right\rangle=\left\{ xa\ |\ x\in  A \right\} .$$

> [!example]
> ##### 1.) $A=\mathbb{Z}$
> - so $\left\langle a \right\rangle=\left\{ na\ |\ n\in \mathbb{Z} \right\}=\left\{ ...-2a, -a, 0, a, 2a, ... \right\}$
> ##### 2.) $A=\mathbb{Q}$
> - so $\left\langle a \right\rangle=\left\{ \frac{m}{n}a\ |\ \frac{m}{n}\in \mathbb{Q} \right\}$
> 	- $=\left\{ \frac{ma}{n}\ |\ \frac{m}{n}\in \mathbb{Q} \right\}$
> 	- $=\mathbb{Q}.$
> - Since every element $\frac{r}{s}\in\mathbb{Q}$ can be written as $\frac{r}{2s}2\in \left\langle 2 \right\rangle$, since $\frac{r}{2s}\in \mathbb{Q}$.
> - Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is a field, it has no nontrivial proper ideals.
> ##### 3.) $A=\mathcal{P}( S )$
> - where $+$ is symmetric difference and $\cdot$ is intersection
> - Let $a\in A$ s.t. $a\subset S$
> - Then $\left\langle a \right\rangle=\left\{ a\cap x\ |\ x\in \mathcal{P}( S ) \right\}$
> 	- i.e. $x\subset S$

> [!proof] Proof: The principal ideal is an ideal of $A$.
> 1. Clearly, $\left\langle a \right\rangle\subset A$
> 2. Closure under $+$
> 	1. Let $xa,ya\in \left\langle a \right\rangle$
> 	2. Then $xa+ya=( x+y )a\in \left\langle a \right\rangle$.
> 3. Contains $0$
> 	1. $0=0a\in \left\langle a \right\rangle$.
> 4. Closure under negatives
> 	1. Let $xa\in \left\langle a \right\rangle$
> 	2. $-( xa )=( -x )a\in \left\langle a \right\rangle$.
> 5. Absorbs products
> 	1. Let $xa\in \left\langle a \right\rangle$ and $y\in A$
> 	2. $( xa )y=y( xa )$
> 	3. $y( xa )\in \left\langle a \right\rangle$ and $( yx )a\in \left\langle a \right\rangle$.

> [!remark]
> - The ring needs to have unity:
> 	- $a\in \left\langle a \right\rangle$:
> 		- Since $a=1a\in \left\langle a \right\rangle$, $1$ is the unity of $A$
> 	- In fact, $\left\langle a \right\rangle$ is the smallest ideal of $A$ containing $a$.

#### Prime ideals and maximal ideals

> [!theorem]
> Let $A$ be a ring and $I$ be an ideal of $A$.
> $I$ is *prime* means $\forall x,y\in A$, if $xy\in I$ then $x\in I$ or $y\in I$.

> [!remark]
> - Let $n\ge 2\in \mathbb{Z}^{+}$ and $I=\left\langle n \right\rangle=\left\{ ...,-2n,-n,0,n,2n,... \right\}$. 
> - $I$ is prime *if and only if* $n$ is a prime number.
> - If $n$ is composite, then $I$ is not prime
> 	- $ab=n\in I$ but $a\notin I$ and $b\notin I$.
> - If $n$ is prime, WTS $I$ is prime
> 	- Let $x,y\in \mathbb{Z}$
> 	- Suppose $xy\in I$
> 		- So $n |xy$ i.e. $xy$ is a multiple of $n$
> 		- Since $n$ is prime, by Euclid's lemma, $n|x$ or $n|y$, so $x\in I$ or $y\in I$

---

> [!corollary] Euclid's Lemma
> If $p$ is prime and $p|ab$, then $p|a$ or $p|b$.

> [!remark]
> Let $A$ be a commutative ring with unity. The trivial ideal, $\left\{ 0 \right\}$, is prime *if and only if* $A$ is an [[integral domain]].

> [!proof]
> - $\left\{ 0 \right\}$ is prime means:
> 	- $\forall x,y\in A$, if $xy\in \left\{ 0 \right\}$ then $x\in \left\{ 0 \right\}$ or $y\in \left\{ 0 \right\}$
> 		- *i.e.* if $xy=0$ then $x=0$ or $y=0$
> 	- this is equivalent to stating $A$ has no zero divisors.

---

> [!definition]
> Let $A$ be a ring and $I$ an ideal of $A$. We say $I$ is **maximal** if $I\not\subseteq$ (but $I\subset A$, so a *proper* subset), but there is no ideal $J$ of $A$ with $I\not\subseteq J\not\subseteq A$.
> The trivial ideal, $\left\{ 0 \right\}$, is maximal *if and only if* $A$ is a field.
