> [!theorem]
> Every cyclic group $G$ is abelian if $G=\langle a \rangle$ for some $a\in G$

> [!proof]
> $G=\langle a \rangle$ for some $a\in G$ i.e $G=\left\{ a^{n}\ |\ n\in \mathbb{Z} \right\}$:
> Consider two arbitrary elements of $G$ which must have the form $a^{j},\ a^{k}$ for some $j,k\in \mathbb{Z},\ a^{j}a^{k}=a^{j+k}=a^{k+j}=a^{k}a^{j}$

- not every abelian group is cyclic
	- e.g. $\mathbb{Q},\ \mathbb{R},\ \mathbb{Z} \times\mathbb{Z}$

#### recall
direct product of $G,\ H$ is $G \times H$ with $(a,b)(a\prime,b\prime)=(aa\prime, bb\prime)$
- or in additive notation: $(a,b)+(a\prime, b\prime)=(a+a\prime, b +b\prime)$
#### Proof that $\mathbb{Z} \times\mathbb{Z}$ is not cyclic
Suppose toward a contradiction $\mathbb{Z} \times\mathbb{Z}=\langle (a,b) \rangle$ for some $(a,b)\in \mathbb{Z} \times\mathbb{Z}$.
##### Case 1: $(a,b)$
- in quadrant $I$ or $III$
	- i.e. $a,b$ have same sign or at least one is $0$
- then $(-1,1)\not\in\langle a,b \rangle$ - check
##### Case 2: $(a,b)\text{ in quadrant }II, IV$
- then $(1,1)\not\in\langle a,b \rangle$, contradiction
#### Question
Is $\mathbb{Z}_{2} \times\mathbb{Z}_{3}$ cyclic?
- $\mathbb{Z}_{2} \times\mathbb{Z}_{3}=\left\{ (0,0),(0,1),(0,2),(1,0),(1,1),(1,2) \right\}$
- $\langle (0,0) \rangle=\left\{ (0,0)\right\}$
- $\langle (0,1) \rangle= \left\{ (0,0),(0,1),(0,2) \right\}$
- $\langle (0,2) \rangle= \left\{ (0,0), (0,2),(0,1) \right\}$
- $\langle (1,0) \rangle=\left\{ (0,0),(1,0) \right\}$
- $\langle (1,1) \rangle= \left\{ (0,0),(1,1),(0,2),(1,0),(0,1),(1,2) \right\} =\mathbb{Z}_{2} \times\mathbb{Z}_{3}$
	- ... $= \langle (1,2) \rangle$
$\mathbb{Z}_{2} \times\mathbb{Z}_{4}$ is not cyclic:
- ...
- $\langle (1,1)\rangle =\left\{ (0,0),(1,1),(0,2)(1,3) \right\}\neq \mathbb{Z}_{2}  \times \mathbb{Z_{4}}$
- ...
- You can show that no other element generates it either and so their least common multiple is *less than* $8$
	- has to do w/ fact that $2, 4$ have common divisor ($2$)

##### Generators
- A cyclic (sub)group is, by definition, generated by a single element: $G=\langle a \rangle$
	- though it's possible that multiple choices of $a$ work
	- $G=\langle a \rangle$, meaning smallest subgroup of $G$ containing $a$
- Instead, we can consider (sub)groups generated by multiple elements (in combination)
	- eg. $\langle a,b \rangle$
	- not in general considered cyclic

> [!definition]
> $\langle a,b \rangle$ is the set of all products of $a,b, a^{-1},b^{-1}$, in any order, with repetition allowed
> - eg. $abaabb^{-1}a=abaaa=aba^{3}$

###### Note
- for $\langle a \rangle$, this is equivalent to the standard definition:
	- The products of $a, a^{-1}$ in any order, with repetition allowed, are exactly the integer powers of $a$
		- $aa^{-1}aa^{-1}a^{-1}a^{-1}=a^{-2}$

> [!example]
> In $GL_{3}(\mathbb{R})$, let $A=\begin{pmatrix} 0&1&0\\0&0&1\\1&0&0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B=\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0\\0 & 0 & 1\\0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Find $\langle A, B \rangle$ and make a table for it.
> ##### Solution
> $A^{2}=\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0\\0 & 0 & 1\\1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0\\0 & 0 & 1\\1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1\\1 & 0 & 0\\0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$
> $A^{3}=A^{2}A=\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1\\1 & 0 & 0\\0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0\\0 & 0 & 1\\1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}=I$
> $B^{2}=\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0\\0 & 0 & 1\\0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0\\0 & 0 & 1\\0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}=I$
> $AB=\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0\\0 & 0 &1\\1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0\\0 & 0 &1\\0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1\\0 & 1 & 0\\1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$
> $A^{2}B=\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1\\1 & 0 & 0\\0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0\\0 & 0 & 1\\0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0\\1 & 0 & 0\\0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$
> $BA=...=A^{2}$
> - in fact, $\langle A,B \rangle=\left\{ I,A,A^2,B, AB,A^2B \right\}$, which can be verified by making a table to observe closure
> $$
> > \left(\begin{array}{c|cccccc}
> & \underline{I} & \underline{A}  & \underline{A^{2}} & \underline{B} & \underline{AB} & \underline{A^2B} \\
> I &   \\
> A &     \\
> A^{2} &  \\
> B & B & A^{2}B & AB & I & A^{2}  & A\\
> AB &  \\
> A^{2}B &
> \end{array}\right)
> \begin{align}
> BA^{2}&=BAA     && &BAB &=A^{2}BB \\
> &=A^{2}BA    && &    &=A^{2}. \\
> &=A^{2}A^{2}B   && &BA^{2}B&=BAAB \\
> &=A^{3}AB    && &    &=A^{3}AB^{2} \\
> &=AB.     && &    &=A.\\
> \end{align}
>
> $$
