## Homomorphisms and normal subgroups
==Recall==: $f: G\rightarrow H$ is a homomorphism means $\forall a,b\in G,\ f(ab)=f(a)f(b)$

*Claim.* if $n$ is a multiple of $m$ then $f:\mathbb{Z}_{n}\rightarrow \mathbb{Z}_{m}$ where $f(a)=a\text{ mod }m$ is a homomorphism
*Proof.* Suppose $n$ is a multiple of $m$. Let $a,b\in \mathbb{Z}_{n}$. WTS $f(a+b\text{ mod }n)=f(a)+f(b)\text{ mod }m$
- $LHS = (a+b\text{ mod }n)\text{ mod }m=a+b\text{ mod }m$ because $n$ is a multiple of $m$
- $RHS=(a\text{ mod }m)+(b\text{ mod }m)\text{ mod }m=a+m$ as well.
- ==note==: this fails if $f$ is *not* a multiple of $m$
	- e.g. $n=5, m=2$ since $\left(\begin{array}{c|ccccc} &0&1&2&3&4\\0&0&1&2&3&4\\1&1&2&3&4&0\\3&3&4&0&1&2\\ 4&4&0&1&2&3\end{array}\right)\longrightarrow ^{\text{replace } a \text{ with }f(a) }\left(\begin{array}{c|ccccc} &0&1&0&1&0\\ 0&0&1&0&1&0\\ 1&1&0&1&0&0\\ 0&1&0&0&1&0 \\ 1&0&0&1&0&1  \end{array}\right)$
==Recall==: for a homomorphism $f:G\rightarrow H$
- $\text{ker}(f)=\left\{ a\in G\ |\ f(a)=e \right\}$
- $\text{ran}(f)=\left\{ f(a)\ |\ a\in G \right\}$
- $\text{ker}(f)$ a subgroup of $G$
- $\text{ran}(f)$ a subgroup of $H$

> [!example] Example: $f:\mathbb{R}^{*}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{*},\ f(x)=\lvert x \rvert$
> $\text{ker}(f)=\left\{ a\in \mathbb{R}^{*}\ |\ f(a)=1 \right\}=\left\{ a\in\mathbb{R}^{*}\ |\ \lvert a \rvert=1 \right\}$
> $\text{ran}(f)=\left\{ \lvert a \rvert\ |\ a\in\mathbb{R}^{*} \right\}$

> [!example]
>  *Claim.* $f:\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}\_{m}, f(a)=a\text{ mod }m$
> $\text{ker}(f)=\left\{ a\in \mathbb{Z}\ |\ f(a)=0 \right\}=\left\{ a\in \mathbb{Z}\ |\ a\text{ mod }m=0 \right\}=\left\{ a\in \mathbb{Z}\ |\ a\text{ is a multiple of m} \right\}=\left\langle m \right\rangle$
> $\text{ran}(f)=\mathbb{Z}\_{m}$

> [!example]
> $\text{det}:GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{*}$ is a homomorphism:
> 1. $\text{det}(AB)=\text{det}(A)\text{det}(B)$
> 2. $\text{ker}(\text{det})=\left\{ A\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})\ |\ \text{det}(A)=1 \right\}=SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})$

Let $G, H$ be groups such that $f:G \times H\rightarrow G$ and $f(a,b)=a$. We show that this is a homomorphism: $\forall(a,b), (a', b')\in G \times H$:
- $f\left( (a,b)(a', b') \right)=f((aa', bb'))=aa'$
- $f((a,b))f((a',b'))=aa'$ also works
- $\text{ker}(f)=\left\{ (a,b)\in G \times H\ |\ f((a,b))=e \right\}=\left\{ (e,b)\ |\ \left\{ e \right\} \times H \right\}$
==note==: let $f: G_{1}\rightarrow G_{2}$ be a homomorphism. Let $a\in \text{ker}(f)$ and $x\in G_{1}$. Then $xax^{-1}$ and $x^{-1}ax$ are in $\text{ker}(f)$
- *pf.* $f(xax^{-1})=f(x)f(a)f(x^{-1})=f(x)ef(x^{-1})=f(x)f(x^{-1})=f(x)f(x)^{-1}=e.$

> [!definition]
> The expression $xax^{-1}$ is called a **conjugate** of $a$. It can be written as $x^{-1}ax=(x^{-1})a(x^{-1})^{-1}=yay^{-1}$ where $y=x^{-1}$, so $x^{-1}ax$ is also conjugate of $a$. So kernels are closed under conjugates.

> [!definition]
> A **normal subgroup** of $G$ is a subgroup $H$ that is closed under conjugates: $$\forall a \in H, \ x\in G\ \ \  xax^{-1}\in H $$

> [!theorem]
> If $f: G_{1}\rightarrow G_{2}$ is a homomorphism, then $\text{ker}(f)$ is a *normal subgroup* of $G_{1}$.

> [!example] Examples of normal subgroups
> *Claim.* $SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})$ is a normal subgroup of $GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})$ 
> - Its the kernel of the determinant homomorphism, which we can check directly:
>
> *Proof.* Let $A\in SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})$ and $x\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})$. WTS $XAX^{-1}\in SL_{n}(\mathbb{R}):$
> - $\text{det}(XAX^{-1})=\text{det}(X)\text{det}(A)\text{det}(X)^{-1}$
> 	- $=\text{det}(X)\cdot1\cdot \text{det}(X)^{-1}=1.$
>
> *Claim*. If $G$ is abelian, then every subgroup $H$ of $G$ is normal
> *Proof.* Let $a\in H$ and $x\in G$. 
> - $xax^{-1}=xx^{-1}a=ea=a\in H$
>
> *Claim.* $A_{n}=\left\{ \sigma\in S_{n}\ |\ \sigma\text{ is even} \right\}$ is a normal subgroup of $S_{n}$:
> *Proof.* Let $a\in A_{n}$ and $x\in S_{n}$. WTS $xax^{-1}\in A_{n}$
> - $a=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{k}$ where $k$ is even
> - $x=\tau_{1}'\cdots\tau_{n}'$ where $n\geq 0$
> - $x^{-1}=\tau_{n}'\cdots\tau_{1}'^{-1}=\tau_{n}'\cdots\tau_{1}'$
> - So $xax^{-1}=\tau_{1}'\cdots\tau_{n}'\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{k}\tau_{n}'\cdots\tau_{1}'$, the product of $2n+k$ transpositions, which is also even (since $k$ is even)

==note==: Another way to show $A_{n}$ is normal in $S_{n}$ is to show it is the kernel of $f: S_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{2}$ where $$f(\sigma)=\begin{cases} 0&&\text{if }\sigma\text{ is even} \\ 1&&\text{if }\sigma\text{ is odd}\end{cases}$$... and show $f$ is a homomorphism.

> [!example] Examples of non-normal subgroups
> *Claim.* $H=\left\langle (1,2) \right\rangle=\left\{ e,(1,2) \right\}$ is not a normal subgroup
> *Proof.* $(1\ 2)\in H$, but $(1\ 2\ 3)(1\ 2)(1\ 2\ 3)^{-1}=(1\ 2\ 3)(1\ 2)(3\ 2\ 1)=(2\ 3)\not\in H$
