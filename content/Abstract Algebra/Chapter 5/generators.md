> [!definition]
> A group $G$ is **cyclic** if $G=\langle a \rangle$ for some $a\in G$, and we say $a$ is a *generator* of $G$.

- the single element by which all elements are created in a [[subgroups|subgroup]] under some operation
- sometimes multiple choices of $a$ work
	- then, we can consider (sub)groups generated by multiple elements (in combination)
		- such as $\left\langle a,b \right\rangle$
		- typically not considered cyclic

> [!corollary]
> $\langle a,b \rangle$ is the set of all products of $a,b, a^{-1},b^{-1}$, in any order, with repetition allowed
> - eg. $abaabb^{-1}a=abaaa=aba^{3}$

> [!remark]
> For $\langle a \rangle$, this is equivalent to the standard definition:
> - The products of $a, a^{-1}$ in any order, with repetition allowed, are exactly the integer powers of $a$: $aa^{-1}aa^{-1}a^{-1}a^{-1}=a^{-2}$

> [!example]
> - $(\mathbb{Z}_{10},+_{10})$ is cyclic (generated by $1$)
> 	- $3, 7, 9$ also works
> - $(\mathbb{Z}_{n}, +_{n})$ is cyclic (generated by $1$)
> 	- $n-1$ also works
> 	- may also be other generators as well
> - $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ itself is cyclic (generated by $1$, $-1$)
> 	- no other generators
> 	- $\langle 1 \rangle=\left\{ ...-2,-1,0,1,2,... \right\}=\langle -1 \rangle=\mathbb{Z}$

> [!example] Example $\rightarrow$ Non-Cyclic Groups
> - $GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})$ and $SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})$:
> 	- these are uncountable, whereas cyclic groups are countable
> 	- non-abelian and aren't commutative, whereas cyclic groups are abelian
> - $(\mathbb{Q}, +)$, $(\mathbb{R}, +)$
> 	- Proof by supposing toward a contradiction: $\mathbb{Q}=\langle a \rangle=\langle ..., -2a, -a, 0, a, 2a, ... \rangle$ for some $a\in \mathbb{Q}$. But $\frac{a}{2}\in \mathbb{Q}-\langle a \rangle$, a contradiction.

> [!example]
> In $GL_{3}(\mathbb{R})$, let $A=\begin{pmatrix} 0&1&0\\0&0&1\\1&0&0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B=\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0\\0 & 0 & 1\\0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Find $\langle A, B \rangle$ and make a table for it.
> ##### Solution
> $A^{2}=\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0\\0 & 0 & 1\\1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0\\0 & 0 & 1\\1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1\\1 & 0 & 0\\0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$
> $A^{3}=A^{2}A=\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1\\1 & 0 & 0\\0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0\\0 & 0 & 1\\1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}=I$
> $B^{2}=\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0\\0 & 0 & 1\\0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0\\0 & 0 & 1\\0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}=I$
> $AB=\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0\\0 & 0 &1\\1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0\\0 & 0 &1\\0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1\\0 & 1 & 0\\1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$
> $A^{2}B=\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1\\1 & 0 & 0\\0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0\\0 & 0 & 1\\0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0\\1 & 0 & 0\\0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$
> $BA=...=A^{2}$
> - in fact, $\langle A,B \rangle=\left\{ I,A,A^2,B, AB,A^2B \right\}$, which can be verified by making a table to observe closure
> $$
> > \left(\begin{array}{c|cccccc}
> & \underline{I} & \underline{A}  & \underline{A^{2}} & \underline{B} & \underline{AB} & \underline{A^2B} \\
> I &   \\
> A &     \\
> A^{2} &  \\
> B & B & A^{2}B & AB & I & A^{2}  & A\\
> AB &  \\
> A^{2}B &
> \end{array}\right)
> \begin{align}
> BA^{2}&=BAA     && &BAB &=A^{2}BB \\
> &=A^{2}BA    && &    &=A^{2}. \\
> &=A^{2}A^{2}B   && &BA^{2}B&=BAAB \\
> &=A^{3}AB    && &    &=A^{3}AB^{2} \\
> &=AB.     && &    &=A.\\
> \end{align}
>
> $$
